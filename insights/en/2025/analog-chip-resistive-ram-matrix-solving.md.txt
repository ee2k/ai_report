Published in Nature Electronics, this work presents a precise and scalable analogue matrix-equation solver designed to overcome a long-standing accuracy bottleneck in analogue computing. Led by a Peking University team, the work combines an innovative iterative algorithm with resistive random-access memory (RRAM) hardware to provide an energy-efficient, high-throughput solution for large-scale matrix equations.

**Core technologies and innovations**

1.  **Overcoming the analogue-computing accuracy bottleneck:** Traditional analogue computing struggles with high-precision tasks because of its inherent accuracy limits. The team developed an iterative algorithm that combines analogue low-precision matrix inversion (LP-INV) with analogue high-precision matrix–vector multiplication (HP-MVM). This mixed-precision strategy substantially improves overall accuracy, bringing it closer to digital computing.

2.  **RRAM-based hardware:** The LP-INV and HP-MVM operations were implemented on a foundry-fabricated, 3-bit RRAM chip. Its 1T1R (one-transistor, one-resistor) cells provide eight conductance levels, increasing storage and computing density. The TaOx-based RRAM array is integrated on a 40-nm CMOS platform and precisely programmed with an ASAP (Adaptive Step-size Adjustment Programming) write-and-verify method. A 1-Mb RRAM chip performs HP-MVM, while an 8×8 array performs LP-INV.

3.  **Scalability through the BlockAMC algorithm:** To solve large matrix equations, the team introduced BlockAMC. It divides a large matrix into sub-blocks distributed across RRAM arrays, allowing inversion and matrix–vector multiplication without repeated programming. A sequence of LP-INV and HP-MVM operations lets BlockAMC handle growing matrices, with computational complexities of O(N^1.59) and O(N^2), respectively.

**Performance and applications**

*   **Outstanding performance:** Benchmarks show that, at the same accuracy, the analogue method delivers 1,000× the throughput and 100× the energy efficiency of state-of-the-art digital processors.
*   **High accuracy and transient response:** The method experimentally inverted a 16×16 real-valued matrix at 24-bit fixed-point precision, comparable to 32-bit floating point (FP32). The analogue inversion circuit converged in 120 ns and the matrix–vector multiplication circuit in 60 ns.
*   **Practical potential:** Applied to signal detection in large multiple-input multiple-output (MIMO) systems, the method reached FP32 digital-processor performance after only three iterations. This addresses the accuracy bottleneck in reliable analogue signal processing, with particular promise for large-scale MIMO processing in the 6G era.

**Conclusion**

By combining an innovative iterative algorithm with RRAM hardware, this research overcomes long-standing accuracy and scalability challenges in analogue computing and provides an efficient, high-throughput approach to large-scale matrix equations. The Peking University team’s work advances analogue computing and points to new directions for future high-performance computing and AI hardware.
